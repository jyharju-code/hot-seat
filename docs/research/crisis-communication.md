# Crisis communication research for the debrief coach

Every source below was opened in this run (publisher or Crossref or OpenAlex
record, journal page, or full text). Sources that could not be opened were
left out. Each entry gives the finding in 2 to 4 sentences and what it means
for a spoken interview with a reporter. Findings with weak or mixed evidence
are marked as such.

## Crisis types and matching the response to responsibility

**Coombs, W. T. (2007). Protecting Organization Reputations During a Crisis:
The Development and Application of Situational Crisis Communication Theory.
*Corporate Reputation Review*, 10(3), 163 to 176.**
DOI: [10.1057/palgrave.crr.1550049](https://doi.org/10.1057/palgrave.crr.1550049)
(opened via Crossref and OpenAlex records for this DOI).

Situational Crisis Communication Theory (SCCT) groups crises into victim,
accidental, and preventable clusters, in rising order of how much
responsibility audiences attribute to the organisation. The stronger the
attributed responsibility, the more accommodative the response must be:
denial fits only low responsibility, while apology and corrective action fit
high responsibility, and instructing information (how people protect
themselves) plus adjusting information (how they cope) belong in every
response. In a spoken interview this means the owner first has to judge
honestly how responsible listeners will think the business is, then match
the tone: a preventable failure answered with excuses reads as evasion.

**Bradford, J. L., and Garrett, D. E. (1995). The Effectiveness of Corporate
Communicative Responses to Accusations of Unethical Behavior. *Journal of
Business Ethics*, 14(11), 875 to 892.**
DOI: [10.1007/BF00882067](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00882067)
(opened via Crossref record for this DOI).

This experiment compared responses to accusations of unethical behaviour
and is one of the early tests behind the SCCT claim that denial is risky
when audiences believe the accusation. It is cited inside Coombs (2007) and
Coombs and Holladay (2008) for the point that communicative responses
differ in how much responsibility they accept, and that acceptance matters
when evidence of fault is strong. Evidence note: a single older vignette
experiment, so treat the size of the effect as mixed, the direction as
consistent with later SCCT work. In an interview, denying a fact the
reporter can document is the highest-risk move available.

## Image repair: the menu of moves and their limits

**Benoit, W. L. (1997). Image Repair Discourse and Crisis Communication.
*Public Relations Review*, 23(2), 177 to 186.**
DOI: [10.1016/S0363-8111(97)90023-0](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0363-8111(97)90023-0)
(opened via Crossref record for this DOI).

Benoit lists five families of image repair: denial (simple denial or
shifting the blame), evasion of responsibility (provocation, defeasibility
or lack of information, accident, good intentions), reducing offensiveness
(bolstering, minimisation, differentiation, transcendence, attacking the
accuser, compensation), corrective action, and mortification (admitting
fault and asking forgiveness). The theory is a typology, not an effect
study, so it tells the coach what move an answer is, not that the move
works. In a spoken interview, naming the move helps the debrief: "that was
blame-shifting" is checkable against the quote in a way "bad answer" is not.

**Benoit, W. L., and Drew, S. (1997). Appropriateness and Effectiveness of
Image Repair Strategies. *Communication Reports*, 10(2), 153 to 163.**
DOI: [10.1080/08934219709367671](https://doi.org/10.1080/08934219709367671)
(opened via Crossref record for this DOI).

This companion piece asks which strategies audiences judge appropriate and
effective, and it underpins the warning that mortification and corrective
action are judged more appropriate when the actor is clearly responsible,
while denial and evasion fit only when responsibility is genuinely in
doubt. Evidence note: perception ratings from the 1990s, so direction is
useful and effect sizes should not be quoted. For the owner, the practical
line is: if you did it, do not spend interview time on moves designed for
people who did not.

## Stealing thunder: disclosing bad news yourself

**Arpan, L. M., and Roskos-Ewoldsen, D. R. (2005). Stealing Thunder:
Analysis of the Effects of Proactive Disclosure of Crisis Information.
*Public Relations Review*, 31(3), 425 to 433.**
DOI: [10.1016/j.pubrev.2005.05.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pubrev.2005.05.003)
(opened via Crossref and OpenAlex records, and as quoted and tested in
Claeys and Cauberghe 2012 below).

"When an organization steals thunder, it breaks the news about its own
crisis before the crisis is discovered by the media or other interested
parties" (p. 425). Organisations that self-disclosed were perceived as
more credible than organisations where an outside source broke the news
first. This is the research name for what Hot Seat scores as
"disclosed_proactively": an owner-known landmine raised by the owner,
before the reporter raises it, buys credibility that the same fact dragged
out later does not.

**Claeys, A.-S., and Cauberghe, V. (2012). Crisis Response and Crisis
Timing Strategies, Two Sides of the Same Coin. *Public Relations Review*,
38(1), 83 to 88.**
DOI: [10.1016/j.pubrev.2011.09.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pubrev.2011.09.001)
(full text opened).

In a 2 by 2 experiment with 137 participants, timing moderated content:
organisations that did not steal thunder needed a reputation-restoring
response (such as an apology) and did worse with bare objective
information, while organisations that stole thunder did not need the
explicit apology on top for the same reputational outcome. The authors
also note the legal tension: an explicit apology can open an organisation
to lawsuits, which is one reason timing is valuable. In interview terms,
volunteering the bad fact early is not just brave, it changes what the
rest of the answer has to carry.

## What makes an apology credible, and its legal side

**Coombs, W. T., and Holladay, S. J. (2008). Comparing Apology to
Equivalent Crisis Response Strategies: Clarifying Apology's Role and Value
in Crisis Communication. *Public Relations Review*, 34(3), 252 to 257.**
DOI: [10.1016/j.pubrev.2008.04.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pubrev.2008.04.001)
(opened via Crossref record; finding also stated in Claeys and Cauberghe
2012, full text opened).

Apology is not magic on its own: compensation and sympathy performed as
well as apology at signalling that the organisation accepts responsibility,
because all three focus on the victim's needs rather than the
organisation's image. The same literature notes the legal worry that a
full apology may be treated as an admission, which is why the victim-focused
alternatives matter. Spoken version: "I am sorry this happened to you, here
is what we have done for the people affected" carries most of the weight;
a bare "sorry" with nothing for victims carries little.

**Lewicki, R. J., Polin, B., and Lount, R. B. (2016). An Exploration of
the Structure of Effective Apologies. *Negotiation and Conflict Management
Research*, 9(2), 177 to 196.**
DOI: [10.1111/ncmr.12073](https://doi.org/10.1111/ncmr.12073)
(journal article page opened at
[ncmr.lps.library.cmu.edu/article/id/264](https://ncmr.lps.library.cmu.edu/article/id/264/)).

Across two studies (755 participants in total, as reported on the journal
page), apologies with more of six components were rated more effective,
credible, and adequate, and acknowledgement of responsibility was the most
important component, with a request for forgiveness the least important.
Apologies also worked better after competence failures than after
integrity failures, which is a warning that "sorry" does not repair a
honesty problem. In an interview the credible order is: name what you did,
own it, show regret, explain briefly, offer repair, and only then move on.

**Robbennolt, J. K. (2003). Apologies and Legal Settlement: An Empirical
Examination. *Michigan Law Review*, 102(3), 460 to 535.**
DOI: [10.2307/3595367](https://doi.org/10.2307/3595367)
(opened via Crossref record; summary of findings opened in the Victorian
Ombudsman apologies guide below).

In Robbennolt's experiments, a full apology (sympathy plus accepting
responsibility) made injured parties more likely to accept a settlement
than a partial apology (sympathy only) or no apology, and a partial
apology could leave people more uncertain than silence. Evidence note:
vignette experiments about settlement decisions, not live interviews, so
the legal-settlement numbers do not transfer directly, but the
full-versus-partial distinction is consistent with Lewicki et al. (2016).
For a small-business owner the lesson is that "sorry you feel that way"
is the worst of both worlds: it sounds evasive and repairs nothing.

**Victorian Ombudsman (2017). Apologies: Good Practice Guide.**
URL: [ombudsman.vic.gov.au/learn-from-us/practice-guides/apologies-guide](https://www.ombudsman.vic.gov.au/learn-from-us/practice-guides/apologies-guide/)
(page opened).

This practice guide, which summarises Robbennolt (2003) and related work,
notes that organisations often withhold apologies from fear of legal
exposure, and recommends law reform so that an apology is not usable as
an admission of liability; it frames an effective apology as recognition,
responsibility, regret, reasons, redress, and release. Evidence note:
this is a government practice guide synthesising research, not a primary
study. For the debrief it supports one careful line: legal risk is a real
reason owners hesitate, and the coach should praise victim-focused redress
without demanding a legal admission the owner cannot safely make. Owners
with live legal exposure need a lawyer, not a script.

## "No comment", silence, speculation, and speed

**Reynolds Journalism Institute and SmithGeiger (2026). No Comment: The
Importance of Transparency in American Journalism.**
URL: [rjionline.org news article](https://rjionline.org/news/journalism-must-retire-no-comment-phrase-new-survey-from-reynolds-journalism-institute-reveals/)
(article opened).

In this national survey, 39 percent of audience respondents said "no
comment" indicates the source is hiding something, 20 percent said they
trust such stories less, and nine in ten reporters surveyed had received a
no-comment response in the previous three years. An embedded experiment
found audiences rated the wording "the story will be updated when we hear
back" as more credible than "no comment" on every measure used. Evidence
note: a 2026 industry survey with an embedded wording experiment, useful
for audience perception of the phrase, not a controlled crisis experiment,
so treat it as weak-to-mixed evidence. Interview translation: never say
the words "no comment"; say what you can say and when you will update.

**Eriksson, M. (2018). Lessons for Crisis Communication on Social Media:
A Systematic Review of What Research Tells the Practice. *International
Journal of Strategic Communication*, 12(5), 526 to 551.**
DOI: [10.1080/1553118X.2018.1510405](https://doi.org/10.1080/1553118X.2018.1510405)
(full text opened).

Reviewing 104 peer-reviewed articles and conference papers from 2004 to
2017, Eriksson finds the recurring lessons are dialogue, choosing the
right message, source, and timing, precrisis preparation, monitoring, and
still prioritising traditional media during a crisis. The review is
explicit that the evidence base is mostly United States and Twitter
research, which limits how far the lessons travel. For Hot Seat it
supports two scoring habits: the first response should come fast and from
the owner (right source, right timing), and a spoken interview still
counts as the traditional-media moment even when clips travel online.

On speculation specifically, this review and the SCCT base response
(instructing information) support the same rule from opposite directions:
say what people should do and what is confirmed, and do not fill gaps with
guesses about cause or blame. No opened source provided a controlled
effect size for speculating in an interview, so the coach should treat
"do not speculate" as a well-supported practice rule with mixed direct
evidence, and score the visible harm: a guess the reporter can falsify
becomes the headline quote.

## Media-interview practice: bridging and message discipline

**Toronto Metropolitan University, Ted Rogers School of Management
(n.d.). Acing Your Media Interview.**
URL: [torontomu.ca media interview guide](https://www.torontomu.ca/trsm-marketing-resources/support-services/acing-your-media-interview/)
(page opened).

This university media guide advises preparing three key messages for the
audiences you want to reach, weaving them in rather than repeating them,
and bridging from a difficult question back to a key message with phrases
such as "What I can tell you is..." so the answer steers clear of hostile
questions "without sounding combative or evasive". It also advises never
saying "no comment" and using the same bridge instead. Evidence note:
practitioner guidance from a university, not an experiment, so it is
included as practice consensus and marked as such; it agrees with the
research rule that a bridge must first answer or honestly decline the
question, or audiences read it as a dodge. In Hot Seat terms, message
discipline is high when the owner lands their points while still answering
the human question in front of them.

## Small businesses and local crises

**Spillan, J., and Hough, M. (2003). Crisis Planning in Small Businesses:
Importance, Impetus and Indifference. *European Management Journal*,
21(3), 398 to 407.**
DOI: [10.1016/S0263-2373(03)00046-X](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0263-2373(03)00046-X)
(opened via Crossref record for this DOI).

This is one of the few directly small-business sources: it studies crisis
planning in small businesses and, as its title signals, finds planning is
often driven by impetus after an event or met with indifference before
one, rather than treated as routine preparation. Evidence note: the
opened record verifies the study and its small-business sample focus;
detailed effect sizes were not in the opened record, so no numbers are
claimed here. For Hot Seat this is the honest state of the field: almost
all crisis-communication experiments use large organisations, so the
coach applies SCCT and stealing thunder to a local owner as a reasoned
transfer, and the small-business gap itself is worth saying out loud.

## Profile interviews: rapport, flattery, and being on the record

**Brimbal, L., Meissner, C. A., Kleinman, S. M., et al. (2021). Evaluating
the Benefits of a Rapport-Based Approach to Investigative Interviews: A
Training Study with Law Enforcement Investigators. *Law and Human Behavior*,
45(1), 55 to 67.**
DOI: [10.1037/lhb0000437](https://doi.org/10.1037/lhb0000437)
(opened via Crossref record for this DOI and the publisher PDF copy found in
search).

This training study with law enforcement investigators evaluates a
rapport-based approach to investigative interviews, in which a positive,
friendly approach improved perceived rapport and thereby facilitated
cooperation and disclosure. The authors discuss excluding tactics such as
forms of emotion provocation, keeping the method to rapport building.
Interview translation for the profile mode: warmth, compliments and a good
listener are not decoration, they are the mechanism that makes a relaxed
owner disclose more, which is exactly the risk the profile rehearsal teaches.

**Canadian Journalism Foundation (n.d.). Interviewing etiquette: a refresher.**
URL: [cjf-fjc.ca/interviewing-etiquette-refresher](https://cjf-fjc.ca/interviewing-etiquette-refresher/)
(page opened).

This journalism practice page advises reporters to build trust and rapport,
and notes that the right amount of flattery, meaning acknowledgement of a
person's accomplishments or struggles, can help gain a person's trust.
Evidence note: practitioner guidance, not an experiment, so it is used as
practice consensus for the flattery rule and marked as such in the coach
knowledge. For the owner, the lesson is symmetrical: a compliment from a
reporter is pleasant and also a tool, so answer the next question as if no
compliment had been paid.

On the remaining profile topics, no source was opened in this run that
directly supports a rule: "everything is on the record" unless terms are
agreed beforehand, the danger of speculating about other people, and the
long silence as an invitation to keep talking. Those rules are therefore
written in `prompts/coach-knowledge.md` as practice guidance and say so,
rather than carrying a source tag they did not earn.
