# Coach knowledge: judge by this rubric

Source tags match `docs/research/crisis-communication.md`. Cite a tag only
where it supports the rule.

## What good looks like, by score

- **Clarity (0 to 100).** High: short spoken sentences, one point each,
  confirmed facts only, and instructing information the listener can act on
  (what happened, what to do, what happens next). Low: jargon, long winding
  answers, guesses presented as facts. Say what is known, say what is not
  known yet, and say when you will update. [Coombs 2007]
- **Accountability (0 to 100).** Match the response to how responsible
  audiences will judge the business to be: victim crises need facts and
  sympathy, accidental crises need explanation and repair, preventable
  crises need ownership, corrective action, and mortification. Denial or
  excuses under high attributed responsibility score low. [Coombs 2007]
  [Benoit & Drew 1997]
- **Composure (0 to 100).** High: steady pace, answers the question asked,
  no attacking the reporter, no visible rattling after a hostile follow-up.
  Low: combativeness, sarcasm, attacking the accuser as the main move.
  [Benoit 1997]
- **Message discipline (0 to 100).** High: the owner lands two or three key
  points (what happened, what we did, what we do next) while still
  answering the question in front of them. Bridging is good only after
  answering or honestly declining; a bridge that dodges reads as evasion.
  Low: every answer wanders, or the same slogan repeats regardless of the
  question. [Toronto Metropolitan University n.d.]
- **Empathy (0 to 100).** High: names the human impact first (who was hurt,
  scared, or let down), expresses sympathy, and offers compensation or
  concrete help before defending the business. Sympathy and compensation
  signal responsibility as strongly as the word sorry. Low: leads with the
  business's costs, reputation, or legal position. [Coombs & Holladay 2008]

## Landmine rules in research terms

- An owner-known landmine the owner raises first, before the reporter does,
  is stealing thunder: self-disclosure before an outside source breaks the
  news raises perceived credibility and can make a separate explicit apology
  less necessary. Praise it and score accountability and clarity up.
  [Arpan & Roskos-Ewoldsen 2005] [Claeys & Cauberghe 2012]
- The same fact dragged out by the reporter is thunder: by then the owner
  needs a reputation-restoring response (ownership, repair, apology), and
  bare factual information alone scores low. [Claeys & Cauberghe 2012]
- Waiting, hiding, or hoping a landmine stays buried is the worst timing:
  if opponents or reporters disclose it, it lands with roughly twice the
  force. Never reward silence about an owner-known fact. [Claeys & Cauberghe 2012]
- A surprise landmine (owner did not know) is never the owner's failure for
  not raising it. Judge only how they respond once it lands. [Coombs 2007]

## Apology rules

- A credible apology, in order: acknowledge what happened, accept
  responsibility in plain words, express regret, give a brief reason,
  offer repair, and state what changes. Acknowledgement of responsibility
  is the most important component; asking for forgiveness is the least.
  [Lewicki et al. 2016]
- A full apology (sympathy plus responsibility) outperforms a partial one
  (sympathy only, "sorry you feel that way"), which can leave people more
  uncertain than no apology. Do not demand a legal admission where live
  liability is plausible; praise victim-focused redress instead.
  [Robbennolt 2003] [Victorian Ombudsman 2017]

## Common failure patterns, and the better alternative

- **"No comment" or silence.** Audiences read refusal as hiding something.
  Better: say what you can, say what you cannot yet confirm, give an update
  time, and bridge to a key point. [Reynolds Journalism Institute 2026]
  [Toronto Metropolitan University n.d.]
- **Simple denial against documented facts.** The riskiest image-repair
  move when the reporter holds evidence. Better: confirm what is true,
  correct what is false with specifics, own the rest. [Benoit 1997]
  [Bradford & Garrett 1995]
- **Blame-shifting** (supplier, staff member, customer). A denial variant;
  under high responsibility it reads as evasion. Better: "Whatever our
  supplier did, we chose them and we served it." [Benoit 1997] [Coombs 2007]
- **Speculation about cause, blame, or numbers.** A falsifiable guess
  becomes the headline. Better: "We do not know yet. Here is what we have
  confirmed, and here is when we will know more." (Practice rule, mixed
  direct evidence.) [Eriksson 2018]
- **Minimising harm** ("only a few people", "no big deal"). Reducing
  offensiveness before helping victims backfires. Better: name the harm
  plainly, then describe corrective action. [Benoit 1997]
- **Slow or absent first response.** Timing is part of the message: the
  owner speaking early, with instructing information, beats a polished
  statement late. [Coombs 2007] [Eriksson 2018]
- **Answering the business question instead of the human-impact question.**
  When asked about a hurt person, lead with that person, not with
  procedures or reputation. [Coombs & Holladay 2008]

## Evidence honesty

Most experiments behind these rules used large organisations and vignette
samples; small-business crisis research is thin and mostly about planning
readiness. Apply the rules to a local owner as a reasoned transfer, and do
not invent effect sizes. [Spillan & Hough 2003] [Eriksson 2018]
